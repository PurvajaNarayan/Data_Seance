1. Human-Centered Design and Control
Core Principle: Design for high human control AND high automation, not trading one for the other.
Maintain human agency: Ensure humans can express intent, receive informative feedback, see progress indicators, and get completion reports
Enable human oversight: Design systems so humans can inspect, understand, override, and rollback AI decisions at appropriate points
Avoid excessive automation: Never deploy "invisible AI" - always inform users when AI is making decisions that affect them; provide manual override mechanisms
Support human mastery: In contexts where human skill-building and creativity matter, preserve opportunities for human learning and exploration
Design comprehensible interfaces: Make system state visible and controls predictable; use consistent interfaces that support user understanding

2. Sociotechnical Systems Perspective
Core Principle: AI systems are never purely technical - they are embedded in social contexts with human actors, institutions, and power dynamics.
Avoid the Framing Trap: Model the complete sociotechnical system, not just the algorithmic component; include relevant human decision-makers (judges, hiring managers, etc.) in your analysis
Avoid the Portability Trap: Don't assume solutions transfer across contexts; understand that "fairness-aware" code designed for one domain may be inappropriate in another
Avoid the Formalism Trap: Recognize that social concepts like fairness are procedural, contextual, and contestable - not fully captured by mathematical definitions alone
Avoid the Ripple Effect Trap: Anticipate how introducing AI will change human behavior, organizational incentives, power dynamics, and embedded values in the existing system
Avoid the Solutionism Trap: Seriously consider whether a technical solution is appropriate; sometimes the answer is "don't build this"

3. Security and Dual-Use Considerations
Core Principle: AI capabilities can be used maliciously; design with security threats in mind from the start.
Conduct red teaming: Deliberately test systems against adversarial attacks, including social engineering, adversarial examples, data poisoning, and model extraction
Practice responsible disclosure: When vulnerabilities are discovered (in your own or others' systems), follow responsible disclosure protocols before public release
Pre-publication risk assessment: For research with clear misuse potential, assess risks before full publication; consider sharing regimes that balance openness with safety
Design secure systems: Implement defenses against adversarial examples, data poisoning, model inversion, membership inference, and other AI-specific attacks
Consider dual-use nature: Recognize that many AI capabilities have both beneficial and harmful applications; take misuse-related considerations seriously in research priorities

4. Technical Robustness and Reliability
Core Principle: Build systems with appropriate engineering rigor, especially for consequential and life-critical applications.
Maintain audit trails: Implement comprehensive logging (like aviation "flight data recorders") to review failures and near-misses
Establish benchmark tests: Use widely accepted tests for verification and validation; participate in standardized evaluations
Continuous data quality review: Monitor for data drift, distribution shift, bias in training data, and degraded performance over time
Test extensively: Conduct rigorous testing including edge cases, stress tests, and monitoring during scaled deployment
Formal verification where possible: For critical systems, explore formal methods to prove key safety properties
Build in graceful degradation: Design systems to fail safely and maintain core functionality when components fail

5. Privacy and Data Governance
Core Principle: Respect individual privacy and handle data responsibly throughout the AI lifecycle.
Data minimization: Collect only data necessary for the specified purpose
Lawful processing: Ensure legal basis for data collection and use; comply with regulations (GDPR, etc.)
Access controls: Implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to prevent unauthorized access
Privacy-preserving techniques: Use differential privacy, secure multi-party computation, federated learning where appropriate
Data quality and bias testing: Continuously review data for quality issues and systematic biases
Retention and deletion: Establish and follow policies for data retention limits and secure deletion

6. Transparency and Documentation
Core Principle: Make AI systems and their limitations understandable to relevant stakeholders.
Document data sources and collection: Maintain "data statements" or "datasheets" describing provenance, selection criteria, known limitations, and potential biases
Document model details: Record architectures, training procedures, hyperparameters, performance metrics across subgroups, known failure modes
Version control: Track versions of data, code, models, and experiments to support reproducibility and rollback
Create model cards: Provide standardized documentation of intended use cases, performance characteristics, limitations, and fairness evaluations
Enable traceability: Ensure ability to trace from specific outputs back to the data, features, and model components that produced them
Communicate limitations clearly: Be explicit about what the system cannot do, edge cases, and contexts where it should not be used

7. Fairness, Non-Discrimination, and Justice
Core Principle: Fairness is contextual, procedural, and contestable - not simply a mathematical property.
Identify relevant protected attributes: Understand which attributes are legally protected or socially sensitive in your deployment context
Test for disparate impact and treatment: Measure performance across subgroups; test for unjustified disparities in both outcomes and error rates
Understand fairness as procedural: Don't just optimize for outcome fairness metrics; consider whether the process is fair, transparent, and allows for contestation
Enable contestability: Build in mechanisms for individuals to challenge decisions, understand why they were made, and have human review
Engage relevant social groups: Work with affected communities, domain experts, and advocacy organizations - don't define fairness in isolation
Document fairness trade-offs: Be explicit about which fairness criteria were prioritized, which were traded off, and why

8. Stakeholder Engagement and Governance
Core Principle: AI development should involve diverse stakeholders and appropriate oversight structures.
Expand stakeholder inclusion: Actively seek input from civil society, affected communities, domain experts, ethicists, and others beyond the technical team
Establish independent oversight: For high-stakes applications, create review boards with external members who can assess designs and practices
Collaborate across disciplines: Partner with social scientists, legal experts, ethicists, and domain specialists - don't attempt to solve social problems with technical means alone
Foster cultures of safety: Create organizational environments where reporting problems is encouraged; conduct internal reviews of failures and near-misses
Consider power dynamics: Understand how AI systems may reinforce existing inequalities or create new ones; assess who benefits and who is harmed

9. Deployment and Monitoring
Core Principle: Deployment is not the end - continuous monitoring and refinement are essential.
Start small and iterate: Begin with pilot deployments in controlled settings; learn from real-world usage before scaling
Monitor for reactivity: Watch for ways people game or respond to the system that undermine its purpose
Track unintended consequences: Monitor whether the system changes behavior, values, or power dynamics in unexpected ways
Maintain human expertise: Prevent de-skilling by ensuring human operators maintain competence for when automation fails
Plan for graceful degradation: Design fallback procedures and ensure humans can take over when systems fail
Conduct ongoing evaluation: Regularly reassess whether the system achieves intended social goals; be prepared to modify or retire it if not

10. Research and Development Practices
Core Principle: Promote responsible research norms that balance openness with security and safety.
Be realistic about capabilities: Avoid overpromising; clearly communicate limitations and uncertainties
Distinguish speculation from evidence: Separate demonstrated capabilities from potential future capabilities
Reproduce and validate: Follow rigorous methodology; make work reproducible; validate findings across different settings
Consider whether to build: Seriously evaluate whether a technical solution is appropriate before investing in development
Share responsibly: Balance benefits of openness with potential for misuse; consider phased disclosure or limited sharing for high-risk capabilities
Foster ethical culture: Promote education about dual-use issues, establish ethical standards, encourage whistleblowing when needed

11. Domain-Specific Adaptations
Core Principle: These guidelines must be interpreted and adapted for specific contexts - there is no one-size-fits-all approach.
Understand local context: Deeply study the specific social, legal, and organizational context where the system will operate
Identify relevant regulations: Know applicable laws and regulations (employment law, criminal justice, healthcare, financial services, etc.)
Determine appropriate formalism: Choose fairness definitions and technical approaches that match the procedural and substantive requirements of the domain
Account for local variation: Recognize that requirements may differ across jurisdictions, organizations, or communities
Plan for context changes: Consider how political, social, or regulatory changes might affect system appropriateness over time

12. Long-Term Considerations
Core Principle: Think beyond immediate deployment to long-term societal impacts.
Consider systemic effects: Look beyond individual fairness to population-level impacts on inequality, opportunity, and social stratification
Monitor for value drift: Watch for whether AI systems subtly shift societal values by privileging quantifiable metrics over other important considerations
Prepare for capability increases: As AI capabilities grow, regularly reassess whether systems designed for narrow tasks remain appropriate
Support broader discourse: Contribute to public understanding and democratic deliberation about AI's role in society
Maintain adaptability: Design systems and governance structures that can evolve as technology, understanding, and social norms change
